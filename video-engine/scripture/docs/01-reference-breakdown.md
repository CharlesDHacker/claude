## 1. The format: a frame story with two recurring characters
Every episode is the same machine:

1. **Cold open, mid-situation, with a shout.** The kid (Nico) and his companion (the Siren) are already *inside* the myth's location: at the Sphinx's rock, in a boat past Medusa's cave, standing in Atlas's beard, watching Alexander pour out the water.
   First line is a yell or a question: "SIREN!!", "Nico! DON'T DRINK!", "OW!", "Whyyyy is he DOING that?!", "...that mountain has a FACE!"
2. **Title card in the first 2–3 s.** Gold spaced serif (MEDUSA / ATLAS / ALEXANDER) with a tiny italic subtitle that is itself a hook ("...don't look", "the king who became a mountain", "the water he poured away").
3. **The companion drops a cryptic line.** "It's tears." "That feather... used to be my ARM." "You're standing in his beard." "She wasn't born a monster."
4. **The kid repeats it back as a giant gold pop word.** "SALTY?!" "WHOSE?!" "BIRDS?!" "HIS BEARD?!" "PEGASUS?!" "THEN WHY?!" The letters drop in one at a time. This is the comedic beat that keeps viewers past 5 s.
5. **Flashback told in tiny sentences.** Each caption is 3–8 words, one idea, one picture: "Medusa was a famous beauty..." / "and her hair, most of all." / "But the sea god POSEIDON wanted her too."
6. **The kid interrupts every 15–25 s**, a cut back to the present: "What prophecy?", "No...", "Did you FIND her?", "The goddess who PUNISHED her?!", "Then who was the son of Zeus?!". Each interruption is a question the viewer is also asking, and it resets attention.
7. **A twist that reframes the opening.** The Sphinx's answer was Oedipus himself; dew is a mother's grief; Atlas only ever had to fear the great-grandson; Medusa's face became the thing that *protects*.
8. **One-word or few-word payoff in big gold type.** "Man." / "IT SANG." / "EVERY SPRING." / "PROTECTS."
9. **A small, warm, funny button back in the present**, often an apology to the myth character: "...Sorry, Medusa." "...Sorry about your beard." "...Good morning, Eos." "Can I have another one?" It lands softly and loops neatly back to the opening.

## 2. Look
- **Hand-drawn 2D, ancient-art palette.** Terracotta, cream, ochre, black: Greek red-figure pottery colours. The duo look like pottery figures come to life (Nico's skin is literally vase-orange, the Siren's wings are vase patterning).
- **Two drawing styles on purpose.** The duo are chunky and cartoony with big white eyes and pupils. The myth figures are elegant, flatter, more "museum" (profile faces, long limbs), which makes the flashbacks feel like legends.
- **Backgrounds are painted flat layers** (sky gradient → hills → midground → ground) with soft light: dusk, moonlight, firelight. Each story has one dominant colour key per act (warm dawn → cold blue night → gold).
- **Life without much animation.** Most shots are a held illustration with a slow push-in or pan, floating dust motes / bokeh, flickering fire, light rays, and small character moves (blink, breathe, one arm). Fully animated moments are rare and saved for beats.
- **Signature shots:** black silhouette on a white flash (Oedipus strikes back); circular "lens" vignettes for memories and reflections; pottery-border frames for the riddle; extreme close-ups of one eye or object (Atlas's eye, the bag, the helmet of water).
- Soft vignette, light grain, warm grade throughout.

## 3. Captions and on-screen type
- One caption at a time, centred, about 15% from the top. White heavy rounded sans (Nunito-like), with a soft dark shadow and no box.
- **Colour-coded keywords:** red/orange for danger ("SNAKES", "curse", "never went home"), gold/yellow for names and wonder ("PERSEUS", "DEW", "blessings").
- Present-day dialogue is tinted (orange/gold); narration is white; quoted speech gets quote marks.
- Names appear as a faded spaced gold serif label near the bottom (THEBES, HERA, OEDIPUS, PERSEUS, INDIA).
- Pop words: huge gold serif with a dark outline and bevel, letters typing on one at a time, slightly rotated, placed over the speaker.

## 4. Voices and sound (what makes it feel premium)
- **Character voices, not one narrator.** The kid is a real child-sounding voice: shouted lines measured at 550–660 Hz pitch, normal speech ~250–320 Hz. The companion is a warm, dry adult female who also narrates the flashbacks (median pitch ~170–200 Hz). There is no deep "documentary" male narrator anywhere.
- **Narration is slow and intimate.** Short sentences, frequent ellipses, about 1.4 s median gap between phrases. Emotional words get space.
- **Continuous music bed** (pitch analysis shows a sustained pad/drone the whole time): soft, modal, ancient-sounding, ducked under speech, with **low "boom" hits on reveals** and a swell on the payoff. Very few whooshes; sound effects belong to the story (fire, wings, water, stone).
- Mastered quietly (−22 LUFS integrated); TikTok normalises loudness anyway.

## 5. Why they perform
1. **The hook is a question, not a fact.** "Why do Sirens have wings?" / "Why is he pouring the water away?" The viewer must stay for the answer.
2. **A familiar point of view.** Nico reacts exactly as the viewer does (confused, outraged, delighted), so the lore never feels like a lecture.
3. **Pattern interrupt every few seconds:** new caption, new shot, or a kid outburst.
4. **Emotional turn in the middle third.** Every story becomes about grief, injustice or loyalty, not just spectacle. That makes people comment and share.
5. **A twist plus a one-line moral** that feels like a secret ("Grief can give you wings. Just don't use them to fly away from everyone still here.")
6. **Series identity:** the same duo, title-card style and colour language every time, so viewers recognise the channel in under a second and binge.
7. **Loopable ending:** the last line calls back to the first.

## 6. What to carry into a Christian version
- Same frame-story engine: **a kid + a wise companion who were "there"**, an in-world cold open, a cryptic line, a pop-word reaction.
- Pick Bible stories with a built-in **"wait, WHAT?" hook and a twist** (a talking donkey, a man who outran a chariot, water poured out on purpose, a snake you must look at to live).
- Keep Scripture accurate: quote or closely paraphrase, cite the passage in the post caption, and let the twist come from the text itself.
- The moral lands the way the references' morals do: one gentle, memorable line, never a sermon. The kid gets the last (funny) word.
